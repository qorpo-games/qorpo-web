Epic AI MegaJam starts this week: prepare for a playable result

Q
QORPO Studio9 November 2026 · 5 min read

Epic's 2026 AI MegaJam runs from 12 to 15 November, with a theme-reveal livestream on 11 November. Its announcement welcomes Unreal Engine or UEFN projects and AI tools. For a small development team, the immediate opportunity is to prepare the workflow now while leaving the actual jam game for the permitted building period.

The dates to distinguish this week

Epic published its announcement on 22 October 2026. We checked it and the organizer's jam page on 9 November. The kickoff stream is listed for 11 November at 11 AM PST, or 2 PM EST. The build period begins on 12 November; the organizer lists the submission deadline as 15 November at 5:59 PM PST, or 8:59 PM EST.

Read the current official jam page for the complete schedule, eligibility and rules. It allows solo participation or teams of up to five people, with no limit on AI agents. Registration and preparation are separate from permission to begin the game. The organizer says to declare earlier work.

This is QORPO's editorial coverage of an external event. It does not announce a QORPO entry, sponsorship, relationship with Epic or endorsement of the studio or its token. The practical preparation exercise below is our own proposal for a team considering a short build.

Prepare a route from idea to something playable

An original pre-jam exercise starts with an empty delivery checklist, not a secretly completed game. Name the person who will integrate changes, the person who will review the running result and the machine used to check the delivered build. For a solo developer, those jobs still exist even when one person does all three.

Make sure the team can explain where work goes and how it becomes a version someone else can open. Tool access, communication and a clean way to hand over a change are useful preparation. Keep the actual jam-game work inside the organizer's permitted period, and refer to the official rules when deciding what earlier material must be declared.

Our AI code review guide explores why a generated change still needs a reviewer. A short event intensifies that problem: several plausible changes can accumulate faster than a team understands their combined behaviour. The integration job should have a clear owner before the first busy evening.

Turn the theme into one understandable loop

Once the theme is announced and building is permitted, a useful first discussion is what the player actually repeats. Imagine a fictional game where a player crosses a short space, makes one choice and sees its consequence. That is an illustrative planning exercise, not a prediction of the jam theme or a QORPO project announcement.

Ask what must be visible for that loop to make sense. If the player cannot recognise the goal, a larger environment may only make the confusion harder to find. Decide which single moment would make a reviewer understand the idea without a spoken explanation from the developer sitting beside them.

Keep the optional ideas on a separate page. A second mechanic may be attractive, but it competes with the time needed to make the first interaction readable. This is a scope decision, not a claim that small games always score better or that any particular plan will win.

Keep a build someone else can evaluate

As work progresses, preserve a known playable version alongside the changing development branch. A useful team question is, “Which version can another person open right now?” That answer should identify an actual artifact, rather than a promise that the latest changes probably still work together.

The organizer distinguishes Unreal Engine build submissions from UEFN private-version-code submissions and asks for a short gameplay video. Follow the route for the engine you choose. Our prototype versus production evidence guide explains the broader distinction between a source change, a running result and a validated delivery.

A proposed review session can be brief. Give another person the current artifact and the minimum instructions they need. Watch where they stop, what they misunderstand and whether the ending is identifiable. Record the observed issue before deciding on a fix. Do not turn one friendly reaction into a fabricated user-study result.

Read prize information with its conditions

Epic's announcement describes category prizes and development continuation grants of up to $50,000 for standout entries. Those are conditional opportunities governed by the official rules. A team should make its preparation decision without treating a possible award as guaranteed funding or a forecast of commercial success.

The announcement also points to tools and learning resources. Review those through the organizer's own links. Their availability does not establish that a particular workflow is integrated, that an agent can do every production job or that the resulting game has cleared its licensing and submission requirements.

The published schedule separates the 11 November kickoff from the 12 November build start. Check the official page and rules for the exact event conditions rather than treating every pre-event activity as permission to begin.
